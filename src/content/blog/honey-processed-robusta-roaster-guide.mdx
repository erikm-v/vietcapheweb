For most of the last century, robusta was the coffee you hid. It went into instant, into cheap blends and into the bottom of the bag. That reputation was earned by how most robusta was grown and processed: picked all at once, dried fast and sold by weight.

Honey-processed robusta is a different coffee. Selectively picked, carefully processed lots from Vietnam's Central Highlands are showing up on cupping tables as sweet, heavy and chocolatey, and roasters are using them to build blends with more body, longer-lasting crema and a real caffeine lift. This guide covers what the process does, how to roast it and how much to put in a blend.

## What is honey-processed robusta?

<Answer>
  Honey-processed robusta is robusta whose cherries are pulped to remove the skin, then dried with some or all of the sticky fruit layer, called mucilage, still clinging to the parchment. Those sugars build sweetness and a syrupy body.
</Answer>

Every coffee seed sits inside layers: the skin, a sticky layer of fruit pulp called mucilage, a papery shell called parchment, and finally the green bean. Processing is the decision about which of those layers stay on while the coffee dries.

- **Washed** coffee has its skin and mucilage fully removed before drying, which gives the cleanest cup.
- **Natural** coffee dries as a whole cherry, which gives the heaviest body and the most fruit.
- **Honey** sits in between. The skin comes off, but some mucilage stays on. Producers name the styles by how much remains: white, yellow, red and black honey, from least to most.

More mucilage means more sugar drying onto the bean, but it also means slower drying and more risk. A thick layer of fruit has to be turned and watched constantly, often on raised beds, or it can ferment in the wrong direction. That care is a large part of what separates a specialty honey lot from a commodity one.

## Why does honey processing suit robusta so well?

<Answer>
  Robusta fruit carries plenty of sugar and very little acidity. Honey processing puts that sugar to work, rounding out robusta's naturally bitter, woody edge into cocoa, nut and honey-like sweetness, with a heavier body.
</Answer>

Robusta and arabica are different species with different chemistry, and that chemistry explains why processing matters more for robusta than many roasters expect.

| | Robusta (*Coffea canephora*) | Arabica (*Coffea arabica*) |
|---|---|---|
| Caffeine (dry weight) | About 2.2 to 2.7% | About 1.2 to 1.5% |
| Chlorogenic acids | Roughly 7 to 14% | Roughly 4 to 8% |
| Sugars in the green bean | Lower | Higher |
| Acidity in the cup | Low | Medium to high |
| Body and crema | Heavy, long-lasting | Lighter |

Chlorogenic acids are a big part of robusta's bitterness, and the lower sugar content means there's less sweetness to balance it. Processing is where you can tip that balance back. As one coffee professional told Perfect Daily Grind, robusta fruit "tends to have a higher brix percentage than arabica, so there are more sugars to work with", and because "you're not fighting the noise of acidity", the impact of processing is easier to taste.

There's also a supply story. Vietnam produced around 31.7 million 60-kilogram bags of coffee in 2025/26, and nearly 95% of it was robusta, according to the USDA. The Central Highlands provinces of Dak Lak and Gia Lai sit at the heart of that. The opportunity for roasters isn't finding robusta; it's finding the small share that is picked and processed for the cup.

<Callout type="note">
  Robusta is graded on its own scale. The Fine Robusta protocol, run by the Coffee Quality Institute, scores robusta against robusta rather than against arabica, so a high-scoring lot is excellent *for what it is*.
</Callout>

## How should you roast honey-processed robusta?

<Answer>
  Treat it like a dense coffee. Charge a little hotter than you would for a washed arabica, give it a steady drying phase, find first crack by temperature and colour rather than sound, and expect to finish a few degrees higher.
</Answer>

Robusta beans grow at lower altitudes and are often less dense than arabica on paper, but their cell structure is more rigid. In the drum, that makes them behave like a dense, high-grown coffee. A roastmaster interviewed by Perfect Daily Grind recommends a higher charge temperature, slightly longer roasts, and often a final temperature 5 to 7 °C higher than for arabica.

What to watch for, phase by phase:

1. **Charge and drying.** Start a little hotter so you don't stall, but don't rush drying. Honey coffees carry dried sugars on the surface of the parchment, which can make them easier to scorch if the early heat is too aggressive.
2. **Maillard.** Robusta builds its cocoa and nut flavours here. A steady, unhurried rate of rise tends to give more sweetness than a fast push to first crack.
3. **First crack.** It's much quieter with robusta and easy to miss. Log the bean temperature and colour at first crack on your first few batches so you can find it again without relying on sound.
4. **Development and drop.** Develop enough to lose any green, woody or rubbery edge, but stop short of very dark. Past that point robusta turns smoky and acrid faster than most arabicas.

<Callout type="tip">
  Cup your first test roasts after a day or two of rest, and again after a week. Robusta's body and sweetness often fill out with a little rest, and an early cupping can undersell it.
</Callout>

## Should you roast robusta separately or pre-blend it?

<Answer>
  Roast it separately to begin with. Robusta and arabica take heat differently, so post-roast blending lets each component get its own profile. Once you know both components, you can trial pre-blending and compare the cups.
</Answer>

| | Post-roast blending | Pre-roast (green) blending |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Each coffee gets its own profile | One profile has to suit both |
| Consistency | Easy to adjust ratios after roasting | Ratios are locked in once roasted |
| Workflow | More batches to manage | One batch, simpler on big roasters |
| Best for | Developing a new blend | Established blends at volume |

Hacea Coffee Source, for example, recommends post-roast blending, warning that in a pre-blend the robusta can come across as "burnt" or overdeveloped, because the flavours that suit a darker blend develop earlier in robusta than in arabica. Other roasters find that drum roasting averages the components out well enough. The only way to know for your blend and your machine is to cup both side by side.

## How much robusta should go in an espresso blend?

<Answer>
  Most roasters start between 10 and 25% robusta. That adds heavier body, cocoa depth and a crema that holds up under milk without taking over. Bold milk blends often sit at 30 to 40%, and Vietnamese-style coffees go higher still.
</Answer>

A few blending rules of thumb:

- **Under 10%** works as seasoning. Drinkers rarely pick it, but baristas notice the crema.
- **10 to 25%** is the classic Italian espresso range, and a good home for a honey-processed robusta in a milk-focused house blend.
- **30 to 40%** suits menus built on big milk drinks, where body and strength matter more than brightness.
- **50% and up** is Vietnamese territory: phin coffee, cà phê sữa đá with condensed milk, and iced drinks that need to punch through sweetness and dilution.

## What can you make with it?

Honey-processed robusta opens up products that are hard to build with arabica alone:

- **A higher-caffeine house blend** for wholesale customers whose drinkers ask for "stronger".
- **A Vietnamese coffee kit** for your café accounts: a robusta-led roast, phin filters and a cà phê sữa đá recipe card.
- **A single-origin robusta espresso** as a feature or limited release, positioned on sweetness and body rather than acidity.
- **Cold brew and ready-to-drink**, where robusta's body and low acidity hold up to dilution and milk.

## How do you evaluate a sample?

Cup robusta as robusta. Comparing it directly to a washed Ethiopian will tell you nothing useful. Instead, look for:

- **Sweetness:** cocoa, nut, brown sugar or honey, rather than flat bitterness.
- **A clean finish:** no rubbery, earthy, musty or medicinal notes. Those usually point to picking or drying problems.
- **Body:** heavy and syrupy, ideally still pleasant as it cools.
- **Consistency:** cup several bowls. Uneven cups suggest uneven sorting or drying.
- **Performance in milk:** pull it as espresso and pour a flat white. That's where most of it will end up.

If a lot passes on the cupping table and in milk, test it in your blend at a few ratios before you commit to volume.
